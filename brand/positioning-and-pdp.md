# Bodology — Positioning & Copy Guidelines

Reference doc for anyone (human or Claude Code) writing Bodology copy: PDP, ads, email, landing pages.
Part 1 is the PDP copy standard. Part 2 is the market positioning it comes from. Read both before writing a line.

---

# PART 1 — PDP Copy Guidelines

### The core insight

**Our buyer is not choosing whether to take inositol. She's choosing whose inositol to take.**

That changes everything about how the page should read. She's already sold on the ingredient — she's read the studies, she's seen the ratio, she's probably taken a competitor's tub for three months. What she's actually doing on this page is comparison shopping between products that all look identical from the outside. Same 40:1. Same white powder. Same claims.

So the page's job isn't to explain inositol. **It's to give her a reason to switch — and the reason has to be something she can check.**

That's why the carob line works. It isn't that carob sounds nicer than corn. It's that we're the only brand answering a question the category refuses to answer. (Go look at Ovasitol's page and try to find their D-Chiro source. It isn't there. Neither is Wholesome Story's.)

---

### Rule one: make her question what she's already taking

The top ad doesn't open with a benefit. It opens with a gap:

> *Most inositol supplements won't tell you where their D-Chiro comes from... 🤔*

That single line does more work than any ingredient paragraph, because it moves her from "is this good" to "wait, what's in mine?" Once she's asking that, she's already halfway to us.

**Every major section of the PDP should contain at least one line that sends her back to her current tub with a question she can't answer.** Sourcing. Fillers. Ratio verification. Testing. Where it's manufactured. Pick a different one per section so it doesn't feel repetitive.

Three ways to open above the fold:

1. **"Most brands won't tell you where their D-Chiro comes from. Ours is carob."** — Straight lift from the winning ad. Safest, proven.
2. **"You already know the 40:1 ratio. Here's what the other brands leave off the label."** — Better for warm traffic who've done the research and are actively comparing.
3. **"Same ratio. Different sourcing. That's the whole difference — and it's the part nobody else publishes."** — Most confrontational, best for retargeting a comparison shopper.

My pick: **1 for cold, 2 for anyone who's visited a competitor page or read our blog.** Cold traffic needs the question planted. Warm traffic already has it and wants the answer fast.

---

### Rule two: name the standard, not the competitor

We don't need to say "unlike Ovasitol." **We set a standard and let her notice who fails it.**

*"We publish our D-Chiro source. Carob, not corn."* — She'll go check whether anyone else does. They don't. We win the comparison without ever running it.

This is stronger than a comparison chart because she does the work herself, and things she discovers are more persuasive than things she's told. (It's also just more confident. Brands that list competitors look like they're worried about them.)

If you do want a comparison block lower on the page, keep it to attributes, not names:

| | Bodology | Typical inositol brand |
|---|---|---|
| D-Chiro source | Carob, published | Not disclosed |
| Fillers | None | Often flow agents, silica |
| Form | Pure powder | Capsules, gummies, blends |

---

### Rule three: specifics only

**Numbers and nouns beat adjectives every time.** 40:1. Carob. No fillers. Pure powder. These are all things she can verify, and verifiable claims are the only ones that separate us from a category built on vagueness.

The moment we write something a competitor could copy-paste onto their own page, we've wasted the line. "Optimal purity" is fine once. "Premium quality," "clean," "thoughtfully sourced" — anyone can say those, so saying them makes us sound like everyone.

**Kill list:** "nourish," "support your journey," "gentle," "wellness ritual," "harness," "empower." Emoji stay in the ad creative and off the PDP below the fold.

---

### Rule four: don't rewrite what's converting

The subscription block goes in as-is: **"Never run out. Subscribe and save 40% off today with MYBALANCE, then 20% for life."**

⚠️ The code and percentages above are one campaign's, not a standing offer — several discount codes are in use. Confirm the live code and value per page before shipping this block.

"Never run out" is the right frame because running out is the actual failure mode for this customer — inositol only works if she's consistent, and she knows it. That line sells the subscription on her terms, not ours.

# PART 2 — Market Positioning

## The spine

Everything Bodology says runs off one argument: **we tell you things the category doesn't.**

There are three pieces of evidence for it, and they're interchangeable depending on format and audience:

1. **Sourcing** — D-Chiro extracted from carob pods. We name the plant.
2. **The COA** — third-party Certificate of Analysis published per batch. Myo assay, D-Chiro assay, 40:1 confirmed in writing, heavy metals, microbials. Live and publicly linkable.
3. **Honest dose counting** — we tell her what a real daily dose costs and how long a tub actually lasts. Concretely: **we define one serving as the full 4.1g (2 scoops — 4,000mg Myo + 100mg D-Chiro).** Many competitors define a serving as half that, so their tub appears to last twice as long as it does at the studied dose. Every servings-per-tub and cost-per-day figure we publish is calculated at the full dose. This is the pillar that makes the arithmetic ads work.

**The carob ad is not an ingredient ad. It's a transparency ad wearing an ingredient ad's clothes.** That's why it wins. Women aren't buying because carob sounds nicer than corn — they're buying because naming the plant proves we know things about our own product that other brands either don't know or won't say. Corn just carries enough baggage in this audience to make the contrast land hard.

Consequence for anyone writing new creative: carob is not a standalone angle to be spun out into its own campaign. **It sits next to the COA as a second proof of the same argument.** One spine, three pieces of evidence.

---

## Language rules for carob

**The winning phrase is "carob pods," not "carob-sourced" or "the carob plant."**

"Carob-sourced" is a category. "Carob pods" is a physical object, and physical objects feel traceable in a way categories don't. Update every instance.

**The single strongest line we have:**

> **Not "plant-derived." Not "naturally sourced." Carob pods.**

That line criticises a category of vague language rather than any product. It leaves the reader with the exact impression we want — everyone else is hiding something, we're the one being specific — while asserting nothing about anyone else.

**Never write an "only brand" or "best on the market" claim.** Not because we're being modest — because it's a weaker mechanism. Carob is the standard commercial botanical source for D-Chiro; other brands likely use it too and simply don't say so. The moment we claim to be the only one, we've made a factual bet a competitor can knock down, and we've replaced our real advantage (we disclose) with a fragile one (we're unique).

**The way to be read as "the only one" without saying it: ask a question nobody else can answer.**

> *"We can name the plant. Ask anyone else which plant theirs came from."*

She concludes we're alone. We claimed nothing. Same for "best" — exacting specificity about our own standard, total silence about anyone else's. Detail reads as rigour. Comparison reads as marketing.

**Two phrasings to keep out of everything:**

- **"More bioavailable."** There's no human data showing carob-derived D-Chiro absorbs better than any other source. It's the same molecule. Use *"It's cleaner, and we can name the plant it came from"* instead — traceability is what's actually landing anyway.
- **"Most brands use corn-derived D-Chiro."** We can't verify it, and most competitors don't disclose a source at all — which is our actual point. Use *"Most brands won't tell you where theirs comes from."* It's true, it's unfalsifiable, and it creates doubt rather than making an assertion someone can argue with.

**⚠️ Always say which price you are quoting.** Our sub price against a competitor's one-time price is not a like-for-like comparison, and a reader who spots that loses trust in every other number on the page.

**The bundle is subscription-only:** $98 billed quarterly = 2 × 45-day tubs at $49 each, a 90-day supply. Quote it as **$32.67/month** — the lowest monthly figure we can truthfully use, and the one that wins the entry comparison. Never write "$49/month"; $49 is the per-tub price.

**Discounts:** several codes are in use. Never hardcode one — the page brief names the code and value.

**Competitor prices:**

- **Ovasitol** — 90-day canister, $82.35 subscribe / ~$87 one-time. No small size exists.
- **Wholesome Story** — 360 capsules, $74.95. Their "serving" is 4 capsules (2,000mg myo, 50mg D-Chiro) — half the research dose. Full dose is 8 capsules/day, so 360 caps = 45 days, and 90 days = 720 capsules across two bottles at $149.90.
- **Generic Amazon bulk** — roughly 32¢/day, unverified 40:1, no published testing. Prices move constantly; recheck monthly or soften to "around a third of the price."

## The competitive set and how to argue against each

| Competitor | Their strength | Our counter | Emotional job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wholesome Story (single) | Looks cheap | Cheaper for the same 45 days: $52 sub vs $74.95, or $65 one-time vs $74.95 | Discovery |
| Ovasitol (single) | Trusted, certified | They have no small size. $82 to find out vs $45 | Lower the risk |
| Wholesome Story (bundle) | Looks cheap | 720 capsules and $51.90 more | Shock |
| Ovasitol (bundle) | Trusted, certified | 17¢/day apart — concede it, then move the argument to proof | Tiebreak on proof |
| Generic Amazon (single) | Genuinely cheap | You can't verify any of it | Doubt |
| Generic Amazon (bundle) | Genuinely cheap | Three wasted months | Reframe the cost |

**Two jobs, kept separate.** Single-tub and first-month creative sells a *small bet*. Bundle creative sells *90 days is when it works, so buy 90 days*. If a bundle ad starts talking about low commitment, it's fighting itself.

**Where we win and where we don't:**

- vs Wholesome Story: we win on price **and** experience simultaneously. No trade-off to defend. Easiest argument we have. (Comfortable at the sub price, $52 vs $74.95. At one-time it's $65 vs $74.95 — still a win, but a $9.95 gap won't carry a headline on its own; lead with the capsule count there.)
- vs Ovasitol at 90 days: we're 17¢/day more expensive ($1.09 vs their $0.92). Say so. Leading with our own disadvantage is what makes the rest believable. Then move the argument to proof — a certification tells you it passed, a COA shows you the numbers.
- vs Ovasitol at entry: don't mention cost per day, we lose it ($1.50 one-time is our most expensive rate). $82 vs $45 is 1.8x and changes the unit of comparison from "how much per day" to "how much to find out." Nobody comparison-shops a $45 first purchase against an $82 commitment — she just tries it.
- vs generic Amazon at 90 days: we're genuinely ~3.4x the price ($98 vs ~$28.80). Concede it in line one. The argument is time, not money: inositol takes ~3 months to show results, so if the ratio in that tub isn't really 40:1, she doesn't lose $69 — she loses the quarter and finishes it believing inositol doesn't work for her.

**Never name a specific brand in the generic-Amazon creative.** "Here's what I couldn't find out" is a statement about our own knowledge. The moment a brand name goes in, it becomes an accusation about their product that we'd need our own lab work to stand behind. As written, every claim is about what *we* publish, which needs no defending.

## Channel allocation

- **Cold traffic:** the arithmetic ads (Wholesome Story capsule-count, both SKU levels). They don't ask anyone to trust us — they hand over a calculation she can check in ten seconds and feel clever for having done. ~70% of budget. These are also the two worth putting real video behind; the 720-capsule pour *is* the ad.
- **Retargeting:** the proof and risk-reduction ads (both Ovasitol angles). Doubt and batch assays only work on someone already considering us.
- **Search-intent / comparison pages:** the generic-Amazon ads. These answer objections; they don't create demand.

---

## Standing operational rules

1. **The COA must be one click from every landing page.** Most of our creative makes a promise about a document. If she has to hunt for it, the ad becomes a liability instead of an asset.
2. **Build unbranded backups of every named-competitor ad before it scales.** Meta pulls trademark-reported ads first and reviews later. We don't want a scaling ad set dark for three days while we rewrite.
3. **Recheck competitor pricing monthly.** Ovasitol, Wholesome Story, generic bulk. Ten minutes, same calendar reminder.
4. **Don't swap a winner on a hunch.** Test reworded variants against the live carob creative rather than replacing it.

---

## The highest-value untested idea

Run the carob creative's exact format with the COA swapped in. Same blue, same photography, same three-line structure:

> **"We publish the lab report. Most brands publish a badge."**

If it performs anywhere near the carob ad, we've confirmed the spine is transparency rather than ingredients, we have a second scalable creative concept, and we can stop guessing at angles.
